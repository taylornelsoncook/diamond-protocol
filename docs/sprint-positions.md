# Sprint and cutting positions: what the evidence says, and starting references for the app

Research brief, 11 October 2026. For Diamond Protocol's Sprint analysis (top speed, acceleration, change of direction).

## How to read this

- **Angle conventions** follow the app: thigh and trunk angles from vertical (+ = ahead of / forward of vertical), shin angle from vertical (+ = knee ahead of the ankle), knee angles as the inside angle (180° = straight), distances in metres from the hip.
- **Elite vs developing.** Almost every published number comes from adult sprinters (9.5–11.3 m/s) or adult team-sport athletes. Our athletes are 10–18 and run 6–9 m/s. Positions (the *shapes*) transfer well; *timings* (contact time, step rate, thigh angular velocity) scale with speed and must be loosened for youth. Where I have scaled a number I say so.
- **Bands.** The app grades A within ±a, B within ±b, C beyond. Nothing in the literature defines A/B/C bands; they're coaching tolerances. I have set ±a at roughly one between-athlete standard deviation where a study reports one (3–7° for angles among trained sprinters) widened for 2D video error (Krosshaug's video work found visual joint-angle estimates off by ~15–20°, pose models by less but still several degrees), and ±b at about twice that. Treat every band as an informed estimate.
- **The blocker.** Page fetches were blocked in this environment, so numbers come from search excerpts of the papers and abstracts, cross-checked against each other, not from reading full tables. Anything marked *est.* in the final table is my estimate, not a published target.

## 1. Maximal velocity sprinting (the 30–40 yard clip)

### The whole-step numbers

| Measure | Elite adults | Developing athletes | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ground contact time | 0.08–0.10 s (26 German elite men at 10.1–11.3 m/s: 96 ± 7 ms) | 0.10–0.13 s typical; team-sport adults 0.10–0.12 s | Weyand et al. 2000 (faster runners spend less time on the ground); Kinematic stride characteristics of elite sprinters, J Hum Kinet 2021, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8008308/ ; Haugen, McGhie & Ettema 2019, Eur J Appl Physiol, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00421-019-04139-0 |
| Flight time | ~0.12–0.13 s (124 ± 7 ms) | 0.11–0.13 s (changes little with level) | PMC8008308 |
| Step rate | 4.5–5.0 Hz (4.56 Hz above) | U16 sprinters 4.35 ± 0.16 Hz, U19 4.49 ± 0.12 Hz | PMC8008308; youth track study (U16 vs U19, MDPI Sports 2026, https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4663/14/8/353) |
| Step length | 2.2–2.6 m | U16 1.99 m, U19 2.12 m | same |
| What correlates with speed | Shorter contact, longer step; vertical foot velocity at touchdown (r = 0.77); smaller knee angle at toe-off (less "pushing to full extension") | same direction in youth; contact time is the best single predictor of maximal speed in girls across maturation | PMC8008308; Bushnell & Hunter 2007; Talukdar et al. 2022, https://jses.net/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/Talukdar-et-al.-2022.pdf |

**Why:** at top speed the body is a spring. Weyand's work established that faster runners don't swing the legs faster through the air; they hit the ground harder in less time. That demands the foot arriving under the hip, moving backwards fast, on a stiff ankle. Everything in the five positions below serves that.

### Thigh motion ("whip from the hip")

Clark, Meng & Stearne 2020 (Biology Open, https://journals.biologists.com/bio/article/9/10/bio053546/222783/) found average thigh angular velocity rises linearly with speed (R² > 0.70) and predicts the vertical foot velocity at touchdown. Average thigh angular velocity = thigh range of motion × step rate. In 79 male sprinters at a 60 m max-speed section (Frontiers 2019, https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fspor.2019.00037) the support thigh was **33.0 ± 3.6° ahead of vertical at foot strike** and **−28.7 ± 3.6° (range −37.6 to −15.7°) behind vertical at toe-off**. Faster sprinters show *less* extension behind the body and *more* flexion in front (Frontiers 2025, https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2025.1535798/full), which is the measured basis for Mann and Murphy's "front-side mechanics" argument. Note one study found the opposite direction for several of Mann's variables, so treat front-side as a strong coaching heuristic, not a law (ResearchGate, On the Importance of Front-Side Mechanics, 2017).

Back-of-envelope for a developing athlete: thigh ROM ≈ 95–105° × 4.2 Hz ≈ 400–440°/s; an elite at 4.6 Hz with 110° ROM ≈ 500°/s. The app's 300–380°/s band is on the low side even for youth; I'd move it to about 360–440 (est.).

### The five ALTIS positions (McMillan & Pfaff, *The ALTIS Kinogram Method*, https://altis.world/altis-kinogram-method/ ; summary at https://simplifaster.com/articles/altis-kinogram-method/)

ALTIS defines the frames but, deliberately, gives few angles ("the frames are not meant to show perfect form"). The numbers below combine their descriptions with the measured studies above.

**Toe-off** (last frame of contact). Look for: thighs roughly 90° apart (back thigh ~25–30° behind vertical, front thigh ~60–70° ahead); stance knee *not* locked (elite take-off knee 155 ± 7°, and a smaller angle goes with higher speed; PMC8008308); hips "only slightly" extended; trunk 5–10° forward; the free foot already dorsiflexed and tucked. Faults: "casting" (thigh trailing far behind, heel flicking up: S-MAS scores back-kick when the heel rises above the calf or the shin above parallel; Bramah et al. 2024, Am J Sports Med, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/03635465241235525), pushing to a straight knee, sitting (hip low, trunk broken at the waist). Cues: "step over the knee", "toe up, knee up", "run tall".

**Maximum vertical projection** (feet level in flight, the hip highest). Look for: thighs still ~90° apart, front thigh at or above horizontal (the highest the thigh gets: Mann's "full flexion" ~70–80° ahead of vertical in elite), front knee bent with the shin hanging roughly vertical to slightly forward (the knee angle is in the 90–120° region; nobody publishes a target, est.), back leg folding with the heel rising toward the glute, trunk tall, arms open. This is the "relaxed" frame. Faults: low front thigh (novices show a much smaller free-thigh angle than athletes; 2024 review in Topics in Exercise Science and Kinesiology, https://oasis.library.unlv.edu/scholarship_kin/vol5/iss1/2/), reaching the shin forward early, back leg hanging. Cue: "knee to the sky, foot under the knee".

**Strike** (ALTIS: the *opposite* thigh vertical, chosen because the hamstring of the swing leg is at its longest). By construction the back thigh reads 0°. The front thigh is then 20–40° ahead, with the shin already swinging down and *back* ("negative foot speed"), the foot dorsiflexed. The swing knee is closing from ~110° toward the ~45° it will reach as it passes the stance leg (Mann: full-flexion knee angle 34° in 9.8–10.0 s men vs 46° in 10.3–10.5 s men; secondary citation of Mann 1990, treat as approximate). Faults: shin flicked out ahead (overstride), toe pointing down. Cue: "pull the ground under you", "paw back".

**Touchdown** (first frame of contact). The decisive frame. Look for: shin vertical or within ~5° of it (knee over ankle), the foot landing close under the hip. Measured touchdown distance ahead of the centre of mass: ~0.18 m (7 in) in the best elites vs ~0.28 m (11 in) in slower elites (Mann, secondary); the CoM-to-contact angle "approaches 90°" at top speed (Haugen, McGhie & Ettema 2019). From an ankle landmark to the hip on 2D video this reads about 0.20–0.35 m, lower being better. Stance knee near 150–160° (a stiff but not straight leg); the free knee folded to about 45° with the thigh already past the stance thigh. Trunk 5–10° forward (est.; Mero, Komi & Gregor 1992 and coaching sources). Faults: landing ahead of the knee (braking; S-MAS scores "foot ahead of the knee/shin inclined back"), heel-first, collapsed knee. Cues: "land under the hip", "stiff ankle, pop off the ground".

**Full support** (hip over the foot, lowest point of the hip). Look for: ankle under the hip (0 ± 0.04 m), minimal "vertical collapse" (S-MAS scores extra knee bend or dorsiflexion here), the recovery knee already *ahead* of the stance knee: S-MAS marks a fault when the trailing knee is still behind the glute or the thighs are more than ~20° apart in the wrong direction. The recovery thigh at this frame is roughly 20–45° ahead of vertical (est.). Hip height: ~95–98% of standing hip height (est.; vertical oscillation at top speed is only a few centimetres, and good sprinters rise more in flight and drop less in stance, Young, Max velocity sprint mechanics, USMA). Faults: sitting, a lagging swing leg, hips drifting back (anterior pelvic tilt, S-MAS item). Cue: "hips tall through the foot".

## 2. Acceleration (first 10 yards, steps 1–5)

### What changes from top speed

| | Step 1–2 | Steps 3–5 | Top speed | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Trunk lean from vertical | 45–50° at step-1 toe-off (world-class 60 m finalists: trunk 39 ± 3° *from horizontal* at touchdown, 43 ± 3° at toe-off) | ~30–40° | 5–10° | Walker, Bissas et al. 2021, J Biomech 124:110554, https://eprints.glos.ac.uk/9874/1/9874-Bissas-(2021)-Kinematic-factors-associated-with-start.pdf |
| Shin angle at touchdown from vertical | ~55° (shank 35 ± 3° from horizontal, GC1) | 30–45° | ~0° | same; von Lieres und Wilkau et al. 2018, Sports Biomech 19(2), step-to-step rise of shank toward vertical |
| Contact time | 0.17–0.19 s (GC1 0.175 ± 0.014 s world-class; 150–194 ms in soccer players) | 0.14–0.15 s | 0.08–0.10 s | Walker 2021; Inter- and intra-limb coordination, Biol Open 2022, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9555766/ |
| Flight time | 0.04–0.07 s | 0.06–0.08 s | 0.12 s | same |
| Touchdown point vs hip | foot *behind* or under the hip | under | 0.2–0.3 m ahead | Kugler & Janshen 2010, J Biomech 43, DOI 10.1016/j.jbiomech.2009.07.041 |
| Hip height | low, rising every step | rising | highest, steady | Nagahara et al. 2014, Biol Open 3:689, https://journals.biologists.com/bio/article/3/8/689/1118/ |

**Why:** Kugler & Janshen showed the direction of the ground force follows the body's lean at toe-off almost exactly (r = 0.93): faster accelerators produced forces that were *lower* in magnitude but pointed more forward, with a more posterior foot plant or longer contact. So the shapes that matter are the ones that put the whole body on a forward line through the foot. Walker et al. found trunk angle and thigh separation at take-off explained ~90% of the variation in first-step horizontal power (R² = 0.89) in world-class men. Nagahara's work shows the trunk and shin rise toward vertical step by step; better accelerators rise *more slowly* (hold the lean longer) and a forward-leaning trunk during the first steps predicts faster acceleration (Gait & Posture 2023, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37481884/). Morin and Samozino's force-velocity work makes the same point from the force side: the ratio of horizontal to total force, and how slowly it decays with speed, separates good accelerators (Morin et al. 2011, MSSE 43:1680).

### Positions in the first 10 yards

**Toe-off.** Trunk 40–50° forward at step 1 (world-class 47° from vertical), easing to ~30° by step 5; a straight line from ear to ankle (no bend at the hip), thighs ~90° apart (Walker 2021 names this as the visual coaching guide), full extension of hip, knee *and ankle* on the push leg (unlike top speed, the knee does reach ~165–175°; est.). Faults: popping up (trunk going vertical early), bending at the waist with the hips back, pushing with a bent knee. Cues: "push the ground away", "stay on the line", "drive low and long".

**MVP / strike.** The flight is short, so the front thigh is punched to a "half-knee" at ~60–70° from vertical with the shin folded and foot under the knee; thigh separation ~90° (est.); back leg fully extended rather than folded (the heel stays low: ALTIS and Spellman describe acceleration recovery as "low heel", https://spellmanperformance.com/blog/improveacceleration).

**Touchdown.** Shin steeply forward: 40–55° at step 1, 25–40° by steps 3–5 (positive, knee well ahead of the ankle), foot under or slightly behind the hip (touchdown distance −0.10 to +0.10 m), trunk still 30–45°, stance knee ~140–150° (est.). Faults: foot landing ahead of the hip and knee (the athlete has stood up), heel strike, knee collapsing inwards. Cue: "foot under the hip, shin pointing where you're going".

**Full support.** Trunk and shin parallel (both ~35–45° forward: the "parallel shins and torso" cue Spellman uses), hip lower than at top speed and projecting forward, the swing thigh already ahead of the stance thigh. Fault: hip dropping below the knee line, or the trunk coming up while the shin stays down (a broken line).

Note for clips: a 0–10 yd clip shows steps 2–5 more often than step 1, so the app's acceleration references should sit on the step 3–5 values, not the block exit.

## 3. Change of direction (planted cut)

Sources: Dos'Santos, Thomas, Comfort & Jones 2019, Role of the penultimate foot contact, Strength Cond J (https://e-space.mmu.ac.uk/626016/3/The%20role%20of%20the%20PFC%20-%20R2.pdf); Dos'Santos et al. 2018, angle-velocity trade-off, Sports Med 48:2235 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6132493/); Dos'Santos, Thomas & Jones 2021, J Sports Sci (https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2021.1948258); Dos'Santos et al. 2019, Biomechanical comparison of cutting techniques, Strength Cond J 41(4):40 (DOI 10.1519/SSC.0000000000000461); Donelon et al. 2020, Sports Med Open 6 (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-020-00276-5); Havens & Sigward 2015, MSSE 47(4) (DOI 10.1249/mss.0000000000000470); Dempsey et al. 2007 MSSE and 2009 AJSM (DOI 10.1177/0363546509334373); Koga et al. 2010, AJSM (https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0363546510373570); Jones et al. 2016, Mechanical determinants of faster COD.

**The angle-velocity trade-off.** The sharper the cut, the more you must brake first: approach speed falls and contact time rises as the angle increases (Dos'Santos 2018, 2021). For cuts ≤45° penultimate braking is small and keeping speed is what matters; above ~60° the **penultimate step is the brake** and the plant is for redirection and push. The 90° cut produces the largest knee abduction and internal-rotation moments of 45/90/180 (Dos'Santos 2021), so it is the one to coach most carefully. Faster 180° turns go with shorter plant contact, more horizontal braking in the penultimate (and antepenultimate) contact and a *lower* ratio of plant-to-penultimate braking (Jones 2016; Dos'Santos 2021, J Sports Sci "How early should you brake").

**Penultimate step.** Foot lands ahead of the hip (a large touchdown distance is how you brake), trunk upright or leaning slightly *back* (0–10° from vertical), knee going through a large bend: ~100–120° of range at the penultimate contact vs 20–60° at the plant (Dos'Santos, SimpliFaster interview, https://simplifaster.com/articles/change-of-direction-tom-dossantos/). In the app's inside-angle terms the stance knee reaches about 110–130° at its deepest in this step. Hips, knees and ankles bend together so the centre of mass drops before the plant. Fault: no braking here, which dumps it all on the plant foot (higher ground force, higher knee load). Cue: "brake early, sink before you plant".

**Plant (initial contact of the final foot).** Foot pointed roughly toward the new direction (not turned out), placed laterally far enough to push but not wide: faster cutters plant wider (greater medial-lateral CoM-to-pressure distance, Havens & Sigward 2015) but a wide plant and an opposite-side trunk lean raise the knee valgus moment (Dempsey 2007; training the foot nearer the midline with a more upright torso cut valgus moment 36%, Dempsey 2009). Shin leaning *into* the new direction, roughly 35–45° from vertical when viewed along the old travel line (est.; coaching convention, not a measured target). **Knee flexion at contact at least 30°** (app stance knee ≤150°): ACL injuries on video happen at 9–23° of knee flexion at contact (Koga 2010: mean 23°, range 11–30°; Cochrane: most sidestep/landing injuries below 30°), with valgus and hip internal rotation of 18–39° in the first 40 ms. Trunk leaning toward the new direction (lateral flexion away from it is a high-risk posture: Donelon 2020 framework). Fault: stiff, straight-knee plant with the trunk falling away. Cues: "knee over the toes, nose over the knee", "lean where you're going".

**Deepest point.** Stance knee ~100–120° (est.; peak knee flexion at the plant in 90° cuts sits around 50–65°), hip and knee bending in the same plane, knee tracking over the foot (no valgus), trunk 15–25° toward the new direction. Fault: knee caving in, trunk rotated away.

**Push-off.** Full extension of hip, knee and ankle along the new line; shin and trunk parallel, both leaning ~40–45° into the new direction (est.: the same "line through the body" rule as acceleration); the first step out lands under the hip. Fault: standing up before pushing; a hopping, vertical push.

**Contact time of the plant** (adults, Dos'Santos 2021 direction, values approximate): 45° ~0.20–0.25 s, 90° ~0.25–0.32 s, 180° ~0.35–0.45 s. Shorter is faster at a given angle, but only once the braking has happened earlier.

**ACL cautions for youth.** Donelon et al. 2020 and Dos'Santos 2021 ("performance-injury conflict", https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-021-01448-3) are explicit that the fastest technique (wide plant, stiff knee, big lateral impulse) is also the most loading. The framework they recommend: brake in the penultimate step, limit lateral foot-plant width, keep the knee over the foot with ≥30° flexion at contact, limit lateral trunk flexion and hip internal rotation, land with the trunk leaning toward the direction of travel. Dos'Santos' 6-week technique program in youth soccer improved cut time *and* reduced these faults (Sports 2019, 7(9):205, https://doi.org/10.3390/sports7090205), so the two goals don't have to conflict. The app should grade safety measures (knee at plant, trunk direction) before speed measures.

## Where the literature disagrees or is thin

- No paper publishes target *angles* for the ALTIS frames; ALTIS itself avoids them. Thigh angles at foot strike and toe-off (33°, −29°) and the toe-off knee (155°) are measured; the MVP knee, the strike thigh separation and the full-support recovery thigh are inferences.
- Touchdown distance depends on the landmark (toe, ankle, CoM) and camera; Mann's 7-vs-11 inch figures are secondary citations.
- Front-side mechanics: the Frontiers 2025 data support it; one 2017 reanalysis found the opposite signs for several of Mann's variables.
- Cutting: the plant-foot shin angle and trunk lean numbers are coaching conventions; the measured literature reports moments, impulses and knee flexion, not shin lean. Knee flexion at contact ≥30° is the one number with injury-video backing.
- Youth: no study gives position targets for 10–18 year olds; shapes should be held to the adult targets, timings loosened (contact 0.10–0.13 s at top speed, 0.15–0.20 s in the first steps).
